Data and Activation Functions: Two Small Levers

How to raise neural-network performance without enlarging the model — expanding data with synonym clusters and combining activation functions asymmetrically.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST June 2026 · 14 min read
1-min summary There is more than one route to better neural-network performance, and “a bigger model” is only one of them. This piece looks at two levers that multiply performance without touching model size. First, a technique that expands the words in the training data into their Standard Korean Dictionary synonyms as whole “groups.” Validated across 108 experimental models spanning CNN, RNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and KoBERT, the recurrent-network family improved by an average of 0.75 percentage points and up to 2.68 percentage points. Second, an architectural technique that combines the Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) with the logistic and Tanh functions asymmetrically across layers to ease the vanishing gradient. It yielded an average gain of 1.024 percentage points, with the ELU-Tanh pairing reaching a top accuracy of 0.9600. Both experiments point the same way — before you scale a model up, check the small components that “work by multiplication” first.
Chapter 1. Framing the Problem

The Power of Small Levers

When a neural network underperforms, the first prescription that comes to mind is usually “make it bigger.” Stack more layers, add more parameters, switch to a heavier pretrained model. This direction certainly works, but it is also the most expensive and the laziest fix. Compute cost climbs steeply with size, while the accuracy gain begins to flatten past a certain point. So before scaling a model up, I always ask two questions first: is there a way to use the data better, and is there room to change the small parts inside the network?

These two questions look trivial, but they actually target the points where performance works by “multiplication.” Training data seeps into every step of learning, and the activation function is passed through once by the output of every single neuron. The more repeatedly an element intervenes across the whole pipeline, the more even a tiny improvement, once it accumulates, produces a difference you cannot ignore. Through two studies I led as first author, this piece records how far these two “small levers” actually moved the needle.

Before you scale a model up, first check the levers that are small but “work by multiplication” — like the data and the activation function.

One side is how to grow the data. We expand the words appearing in training sentences into their Standard Korean Dictionary synonyms, group by group, producing richer expressive variation from the same data. The other side is how to change the architecture. By combining different activation functions asymmetrically across layers, we ease the vanishing gradient that chronically afflicts deep networks. The approaches differ, but the philosophy is one — gain performance through composition, not size.

Chapter 2. How to Grow the Data

Expanding Training Data with Synonym Clusters

In natural language processing, data augmentation always carries a subtle problem. With an image, you can flip it, crop it, and change its brightness, and a “cat” is still a cat; but a sentence can lose its meaning from changing a single word. That is why coarse augmentation such as random substitution or deletion is especially risky in Korean. So I chose augmentation that respects the semantic structure of the language: substituting a word with candidates close in meaning, grounded in the synonym relations registered in the National Institute of Korean Language’s Standard Korean Dictionary.

The key is that, instead of swapping individual words one at a time, we expanded words as groups (word clusters). We treat a word as a bundle of the words that stand in a synonym relation to it, and generate expressive variation by moving among that bundle within a training sentence. This way, the meaning of the original text is preserved while the model learns multiple surface forms of the same concept. Because it is rooted in a validated knowledge resource — a dictionary — the risk of an augmented sentence veering off into an unintended meaning is low.

We designed the validation to be as broad as possible. To show that this is not a fluke that works only in one particular structure, we lined up several architectures from different representational families side by side. From the convolutional CNN, through the recurrent RNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, and GRU, to the pretrained transformer KoBERT — crossing data conditions with hyperparameters, we built a total of 108 experimental models and compared accuracy before and after augmentation, condition by condition.

Why dictionary-based. One could also pull synonyms by the cosine similarity of word embeddings, but in that case antonyms or hypernyms and hyponyms easily slip in as being “close.” The synonym relations of the Standard Korean Dictionary are human-vetted lexical grounds, so the reliability of the augmentation is underwritten by the data resource itself. Rather than one of knowledge resources and statistical methods being absolutely right, for a task where preserving Korean meaning matters, the dictionary was the safer starting point.
Chapter 3. Results and Interpretation

Where, and by How Much

The results split sharply by architectural family. The effect of synonym-cluster expansion appeared most consistently in the recurrent-network family. Performance improved by an average of 0.75 percentage points, and rose by up to 2.68 percentage points depending on the condition. In recurrent structures, where representations accumulate sequentially along a time axis, I read the learning of diverse surface forms of the same meaning as having especially helped contextual generalization.

In absolute terms, 0.75 percentage points may look modest. But the character of this gain deserves emphasis. First, it is a gain obtained from data already in hand, without collecting anything new — which means the labeling cost is zero. Second, since it is obtained while leaving model size and structure untouched, it can be stacked on top of other improvement techniques. As the heatmap above shows, the magnitude of the effect varies with architecture and condition, but the direction was, on the whole, consistently positive (+). A near-free lever usually looks like this — undramatic, but almost always helpful.

The caveats are just as clear. This method is strong in languages and domains where synonym resources are well developed. For text heavy with neologisms, technical jargon, or proper nouns not registered in the dictionary, there are few candidates to expand in the first place, so the effect is limited. Moreover, substituting a polysemous word without context can turn into noise, so expansion must always be capped by preservation of the original meaning.

Chapter 4. How to Change the Activation Function

Asymmetric ELU-Tanh Combinations and the Vanishing Gradient

The second lever lies outside the data, inside the network. The wall most commonly hit when training a deep network is the vanishing gradient. When the error signal is propagated back to the earlier layers, if the activation function’s gradient is small, the signal converges rapidly toward zero as the terms multiply. As a result, the layers near the input effectively stop learning. The shape of the activation function, in effect, decides whether the learning signal lives or dies.

The traditionally used logistic (sigmoid) and Tanh functions are vulnerable to this problem, because their gradients fall to almost zero at the two ends where the output saturates. The Exponential Linear Unit (ELU), by contrast, keeps its gradient close to the identity in the positive region and saturates smoothly in the negative region, pushing the output mean toward zero to stabilize learning. Here I went one step further and experimented with combining different activation functions “asymmetrically” across layers. Instead of using the same function in every layer, I mixed ELU with the logistic and Tanh functions according to layer position, arranging things so that each function’s strengths compensate for another’s weaknesses.

The shape of the activation function decides whether the learning signal lives or dies. Instead of repeating the same function, I combined them asymmetrically so that each covers the other’s weakness.

The heart of the idea is to stop playing the game of finding “a single optimal activation function.” Some layers benefit from passing signal through broadly; others benefit from gently pressing the output down. If the demands differ layer by layer, it is only natural to use a different function layer by layer. The asymmetric combination translates that intuition into a design.

Chapter 5. Results

+1.024 Percentage Points, and ELU-Tanh

Comparing many combinations systematically, the asymmetric combination showed an average improvement of 1.024 percentage points over a single-activation-function baseline. Among them, the ELU-Tanh combination was best, at an accuracy of 0.9600. I read this as a division of labor clicking into place: ELU generously preserving the gradient at the front stages to carry the learning signal through to the earlier layers, and Tanh then gently tidying the output around zero.

The appeal of this result, too, resembles that of the earlier data augmentation: it is a gain obtained purely by changing the arrangement of the components, without increasing the parameter count. Because an activation function is a design choice rather than a learnable weight, it pushes up accuracy at almost no added compute cost. That said, the optimal combination can shift with the task, the data, and the depth, so it is more accurate to take this as “the search space of asymmetric combinations is open” than as “ELU-Tanh is always the answer.”

Closing

By Composition, Not Size

The two studies touched different points, but they point to the same conclusion. Synonym-cluster expansion on the data side, and asymmetric activation-function combination on the architecture side, each pushed performance up while leaving the model’s size untouched. The gains — up to 2.68 percentage points and an average of 1.024 percentage points, respectively — are not dramatic. But the real value of gains like these is that they demand almost no labeling cost and almost no compute cost, and that they can be stacked with each other and with other improvement techniques.

A bigger model is always tempting. But smoothing out first the points that work multiplicatively on the performance you end up with — the data that seeps into every training step, the activation function that every neuron passes through — is more honest engineering and usually more economical. The decision to scale a model up loses nothing by being made only after you have pulled every one of these small levers.
