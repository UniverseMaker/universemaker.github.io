Translating Time Series: Forecasting the Future with Seq2Seq

How the encoder–decoder architecture once used for machine translation came to forecast water temperature, power demand, and nuclear generation.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST June 2026 · 13 min read
1-min summary Seq2Seq (the encoder–decoder) was originally devised to “translate” a sentence in one language into a sentence in another. Yet time-series forecasting — carrying a window of past observations forward into a window of the future — is at bottom the same problem: mapping one ordered sequence onto another. Drawing on real projects — short-term sea-surface-temperature forecasting in Korea’s East Sea, short-term electricity-demand forecasting, and reactor power-generation forecasting for Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP) — this piece unpacks the idea of turning a translator into a forecaster. It covers why ARIMA and single-step LSTMs falter in the face of abrupt regime changes and multivariate context, how to feed environmental variables into the encoder for multivariate inference, and the villain that every time-series model eventually meets: phase lag.
Chapter 1. The Idea

Forecasting the Future Resembles Translating a Sentence

In 2014, an architecture appeared that changed the landscape of machine translation. An encoder compresses an entire input sentence into a single “context vector,” and a decoder starts from that context to unfurl a sentence in the target language one word at a time — the so-called Seq2Seq (sequence-to-sequence) model. It reads the Korean sentence “나는 사과를 먹었다” whole, distills it into a condensate of meaning, and from that generates “I ate an apple” word by word.

When I first met this architecture, what came to mind was not translation but the sea. It was around the time I was wrestling with the task of forecasting the water temperature of the East Sea — reading in a fortnight’s records of water temperature, air temperature, and wind speed to sketch out the water temperature for the days ahead. Looked at closely, this is structurally identical to translation. The input is an ordered sequence (a window of past observations), and the output is an ordered sequence too (a window of future predictions). One is a run of words and the other a run of numbers, but the skeleton — “carry one ordered run over into another” — is the same.

Carrying a sentence across and sketching the future are one and the same problem: mapping one ordered sequence onto another.

This analogy is powerful because it is more than a mere figure of speech. Just as the encoder in translation compresses the “meaning” of a sentence, in time series the encoder compresses the “state” of the input window — what phase this system is currently in. The decoder then unfolds the future starting from that state. All it took to turn a translator into a forecaster was to swap the vocabulary dictionary for a space of real numbers; the grammar of learning could be borrowed wholesale.

Chapter 2. The Limits

Why ARIMA and Single-Step LSTMs Falter

So what were the existing methods missing? ARIMA, the classic of time-series forecasting, rests on the assumptions that the data are stationary and that the future is a linear extension of the past. When water temperature rises and falls gently with the seasons, it is excellent. But in a regime change — where cold and warm currents collide and the water temperature swings sharply within a few days — or at the moment a heat wave or cold snap wrenches the electricity-demand curve, the assumptions themselves collapse.

The naive deep-learning approach — an LSTM that looks only one step ahead — falls into a similar trap. If you predict the next single point, feed that prediction back into the input to predict the point after it, and stitch the future together this way, small errors amplify at every step until, days later, the curve drifts somewhere entirely wrong. The more fundamental problem is the scope of context. A single-step model tends to lean too heavily on the immediately preceding state, and so it easily misses the “big picture” that the whole input window is telling it.

Here the Seq2Seq idea offers a remedy. First encode the entire input window into a single context, then decode the whole future horizon at once. Instead of groping toward the future one point at a time, the model first renders a condensed judgment — “this system is in such-and-such a phase” — and unfolds several steps from there. In the East Sea short-term water-temperature task, the encoder–decoder taking multivariate input traced a markedly more stable trajectory through abrupt-change regions than a single-variable, single-step baseline did.

Chapter 3. Going Multivariate

What It Means to Feed Environmental Variables into the Encoder

Time-series forecasting gets interesting precisely when the target variable alone does not determine the future. Water temperature is a function of air temperature, wind speed, and insolation; electricity demand is a function of temperature, day of the week, and time of day; a reactor’s power output is a function of outside-air temperature, cooling conditions, and operating characteristics. How you channel these exogenous variables into the model is the heart of multivariate forecasting.

This is exactly where the elegance of the encoder–decoder shines. The encoder is, after all, a device that takes a sequence and compresses it into context. So there is no reason to place only the target variable at each time step of that sequence. Feed the encoder a multidimensional vector bundling [target variable, environmental variable 1, environmental variable 2, …] at every time step, and the model learns the interactions among the variables as well. This was precisely the approach I took in KHNP’s reactor power-generation task — combining the reactor’s operating characteristics and environmental variables in the encoder input so that the model learned not simple autoregression but a “conditional future.”

Practical tip. When feeding exogenous variables into the encoder, if some are already known for the future window (calendar information, scheduled maintenance, weather-agency forecasts, and the like), provide them to the decoder input as well. Tell the decoder “the future temperature will be such-and-such,” and the model calibrates its output to that condition. Don’t leave the model to guess at a future it could simply be told.

The short-term electricity-demand comparison study taught the same lesson. When you pit several architectures against one another on the same data, the winner is not simply the more complex model; what decides the contest is how richly, yet how free of noise, a model takes in context. Add variables indiscriminately and the curse of dimensionality follows; cut too many and you blind yourself to the very clues that determine the future. Multivariate design is, in the end, the work of deciding what to listen to and what to let pass.

Chapter 4. Phase Lag

The Model That Trails the Real Curve by a Beat

Work with time-series forecasting models long enough and you will inevitably meet a recurring villain: the phenomenon in which the predicted curve “trails” the ground-truth curve by a beat, or half a beat — phase lag. On the graph it looks plausible. The shape is similar and the error (MSE) comes out small. But look closely and the model has not forecast the future at all; it has merely taken the most recent value, tweaked it slightly, and copied it forward into the future. It always reacts late when an inflection point arrives. At the very moment forecasting is most needed — the moment a trend bends — it is most powerless.

A model caught in phase lag only pretends to forecast the future; in truth it is copying the most recent past into it.

This trap is dangerous because the standard loss function rewards it as a “good prediction.” When the curve is gentle, “nudging the latest value slightly forward” is the safest strategy for minimizing mean squared error, and the model cleverly hits on the laziest answer. What helped? In my experience, three things. First, forcing the model to output a sufficiently long horizon all at once — multi-step decoding — leaves it unable to survive on copying the latest value and pushes it to learn the trend. Second, including the rate of change (differences) or the inflection itself among the prediction targets raises sensitivity to turning points. Third, the injection of known future variables mentioned earlier: if the decoder knows in advance that the future temperature will drop, it flags the point where the water temperature will bend without falling behind.

Chapter 5. Honest Limits

When Not to Use Seq2Seq

Finally, for balance, I want to be clear that this architecture is no panacea. For a problem with short data, simple patterns, and only strong seasonality, ARIMA or exponential smoothing is faster, easier to interpret, and often more accurate. Seq2Seq demands no small amount of training data and tuning effort, and if poorly designed it falls straight into the phase lag we saw above.

Moreover, in domains where “explanation” matters more than the result — regulatory reporting, safety review — an interpretable model takes priority over a black-box neural network. And when the forecast horizon is extremely long (months or years) or the input window must be very long, it is better to consider an attention-based architecture or the Transformer family than a plain encoder–decoder, because cramming all of the past into a single context vector creates an information bottleneck.

Closing

From Translator to Forecaster

The journey by which an architecture born for machine translation came to forecast the sea’s temperature, a city’s electricity demand, and a reactor’s output shows how lightly a good abstraction crosses the borders between fields. The key lay not in imitating the form but in sharing the structure. Whether sentences or time series, the moment you recognize the same skeleton — “carry one ordered sequence over into another” — a tool from one side takes on new life in the other. Even the grand task of forecasting the future may, in the end, be nothing more than carefully translating one sentence, the past, into another, the future.
