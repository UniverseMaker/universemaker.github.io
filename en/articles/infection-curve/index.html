Drawing the Epidemic Curve in Advance: A Forecast Model with Reduced Phase Lag

How a model that trailed rapidly changing case counts by a few days was made to react 'on time.'

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST June 2026 · 15 min read
1-min summary On the Ministry of the Interior and Safety's project "Development of Technology for Predicting and Intelligently Blocking the Overseas Inflow of Emerging Infectious Diseases," I took on two tasks. One was to design an AI agent that assists epidemiological investigators in their work; the other was to build a time-series model that forecasts the number of infections. In the latter, the biggest enemy was not accuracy but "phase delay." If you copy yesterday's number almost verbatim, the error looks small, yet the curve is shifted a few days to the right of reality — useless on the front lines of disease control. This is a retrospective on how a loss function makes a model lazy, how I pursued a model that responds to inflection points while reducing lag, and what I had to choose between accuracy metrics and real-world usefulness.
Chapter 1. The Problem No One Warns You About in Advance

Copy Yesterday's Number and You Get an "Accurate" Model

When I first trained the infection-count forecasting model and brought up the validation graph, I felt a moment of relief. The predicted curve lay almost perfectly on top of the actual curve. The error metrics were small, too. Everything seemed to be working. But at some point, as I overlaid the two curves and scanned along the horizontal axis with my eyes, a chill ran down my spine. The predicted curve did not "resemble" the actual curve — it was the actual curve "pushed" a few days to the right.

This is the most insidious trap in time-series forecasting: phase delay. Intuitively, it works like this. When you ask a model to predict tomorrow's infection count, the easiest strategy it discovers is "just output today's number." Because an infection curve rarely changes drastically from one day to the next, even copying yesterday's value verbatim keeps the average error small enough. The model was not predicting the future — it was reading the past one step late.

A model with phase delay does not predict the future. It merely reads the past a few days late — accurately.

On the front lines of disease control, this delay is fatal. The inflection point where infections begin to surge is the moment you decide whether to add isolation beds, tighten quarantine, and redeploy personnel. But if the model "notifies" you of that inflection point a few days late, its forecast is nothing more than an after-the-fact report confirming what has already happened. A small error guarantees nothing. Only when the curve bends and the forecast bends along with it, right at that spot, does it become useful.

Chapter 2. The Loss Function Rewards Laziness

Why MSE Makes a Model a Beat Late

Why does the model learn such a lazy strategy? The culprit was the loss function we had thoughtlessly chosen: mean squared error (MSE). MSE squares the difference between the predicted and actual values and averages it. Over most of the intervals where the curve moves gently, the "delayed-copy" strategy of simply outputting yesterday's value minimizes MSE. That is because making one or two large errors at inflection points is, on average, worse than steadily making small errors while lagging one step behind across every interval.

In other words, the moment we ask the model to "reduce the overall average error," it converges on the safest average strategy — imitating the past. The loss function never told it what we really wanted ("catch the inflection point on time"); the model faithfully did only what we said ("reduce the average error"). The model was honest. What was lazy was our objective function.

Practical insight. When evaluating time-series forecasting performance, don't look at error metrics alone — also measure the lag at which the cross-correlation between the predicted and actual curves is maximized. If that lag is not zero, your "accurate" model is in fact a model that is several days late. This one line of diagnosis keeps you from being fooled by a small error.

So I reset the direction of the modeling. The key was to move away from a structure that rewards the model for fitting the "flat intervals" well, and instead to induce it to respond sensitively at "the moments when change occurs." I redesigned the training signal to put greater weight on sharp fluctuations and inflection points, and made the model watch not only the "value" of the curve but also the "change in its slope." The goal was clear: not to fit the numbers more accurately, but to fit them on time.

Chapter 3. An Agent That Assists Epidemiological Investigators

Not Replacing People, but Lightening Their Work

The other axis of the project was an AI agent to assist epidemiological investigators. When I first took on this work, I imagined a "system that automates investigation." But as I looked closely at what real epidemiological investigators do, I realized how naive that idea was. Epidemiological investigation is work that happens between people. Asking about a confirmed patient's movements, cross-checking scattered records, inferring the most plausible route among contradictory statements — it is highly contextual work in which judgment intervenes.

What the field needed was not an AI to replace people, but an AI to clear away the drudgery so that people could judge better.

So I changed the agent's design principle. Rather than replacing the investigator, it would slot naturally into the workflow they were already following. It organized the fragmentary information the investigator had gathered into a structured timeline, pointed out in advance the clues in vast records that might serve as connecting links, and searched for similar past cases to present as reference material — an assistant that left the judgment to people while quickly gathering the raw material that judgment required.

This shift in perspective was not merely a matter of humility but of practicality. In a public-safety domain that carries responsibility, the authority and accountability for the final judgment had to remain with people. There is a world of difference between an AI asserting "this movement is the source of infection" and one suggesting "this movement overlaps with two other cases — would you like to check?" The two build entirely different structures of trust. Only the latter was accepted in the field.

Chapter 4. The Tension Between Accuracy and Usefulness

In Public Safety, "When You Warn" Matters More Than "How Accurate You Are"

A tension ran through the entire project. A model's accuracy metrics and its operational usefulness in the field are not the same thing. If anything, the two often collided. Tune the model to respond sensitively to inflection points, and small false alarms increase over the flat intervals. Conversely, make it conservative to reduce false alarms, and its response dulls just when a real surge begins.

This trade-off was not a purely technical problem but a question of value judgment. In infectious-disease control, the cost of a "missed inflection point" and the cost of a "false alarm" are not symmetric. A single missed surge can lead to uncontrollable spread, whereas a single false alarm ends in wasted resources and eroded trust. But when false alarms repeat, the field eventually starts to ignore the alarms altogether — the "boy who cried wolf" problem. So where to set the sensitivity was a matter to be decided not by statistics but by policy consensus.

At this stage I redefined the model not as a "machine that produces the right answer" but as a "tool that supports decision-making." Instead of throwing out a single number, the model would present scenarios together with a band of uncertainty. Not "N people tomorrow," but rather "at the current trend, there are signs of entering a surge phase, and that point is likely within roughly a few days" — information a person can actually use for judgment. Raising the inference performance and delivering that performance in a trustworthy form were two separate tasks.

Chapter 5. Connecting the Forecast to the Field

From Drawing the Curve to the Gateway That Screens People

If the forecasting model and the agent were the brain that judged "when and how to respond," the project also had a physical gateway where that judgment actually took effect — an intelligent quarantine platform. When the infection curve points to a surge phase, the last place that signal must reach is, in the end, the entrances through which people pass. No matter how sophisticated the forecast, if it is not connected to equipment in the field it stays an after-the-fact report. So we widened our view to connect the forecasting-and-judgment layer to the quarantine tunnel and its control system.

It was not enough for this equipment to operate individually. Only when many quarantine tunnels and measurement devices are monitored in real time from a single control screen and operated remotely can the field respond at a unified pace to the phase changes the forecast points to. The KAIST DATA Broker–based IoT control dashboard shown on this article's cover played exactly that role — a bridge that gathered the states of scattered equipment in one place and carried the signals coming down from the forecasting-and-judgment layer into physical response.

Closing

What It Means to Forecast Under Non-Stationarity and High Responsibility

The greatest lesson this project left me with is that good forecasting is not about producing a small error but about giving the right signal on time. In non-stationary data like an infection curve, whose statistical properties change from moment to moment, the ability to fit the past average well can be unrelated to the ability to fit the future well. If anything, the more faithful you are to the past, the more insensitive you become to the future. A model follows the goal we set with brutal honesty. So before blaming a lazy model, we should first suspect what we designed it to reward.

And in a public domain that carries responsibility, the value of a forecasting model is not measured by its accuracy alone. When to warn, who makes the final judgment, how to manage the trust cost of false alarms — a model that cannot answer all these operational questions cannot enter the field, no matter how precise it is. Drawing the curve in advance turned out to be a matter of technology and, at the same time, a matter of judgment and trust.
