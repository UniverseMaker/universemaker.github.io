Predicting the Unseen Pipe: Irregular Time Series, Ensembles, and Language Models

Beneath the city, water pipes that might burst at any moment. We went beyond 'when will it burst' to 'what to fix and how,' tying it all into one system.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST June 2026 · 14 min read
1-min summary A retrospective on the AI portion of a water-pipe safety-management R&D program (2024–2026) carried out jointly by the City of Daejeon, K-water, and AST Holdings. The goal was twofold. First, to predict breaks and leaks in the city's underground water pipes before they happen. Second, not to stop at prediction but to go all the way to proposing a maintenance plan. The biggest challenge was the irregular time series peculiar to waterworks data — missing values, outliers, and non-uniform sampling. We compared DNN, LSTM, Stateful-LSTM, and LSTM-Attention by MAPE to choose a status-prediction model, then bound several recurrent networks together with ensemble voting. On top of that we layered Bi-LSTM, Transformer, and BERT-family language models to generate, in plain sentences, "what to fix and how," and deployed the whole thing as an API on a digital-twin safety-management platform.
Chapter 1. The Unseen Danger Underground

The timetable flowing beneath our feet

A city's water pipes rarely catch the eye. The moment we feel their presence usually arrives with bad news: a road caving in at its center, a perfectly ordinary alley turned into a flood overnight, or the smell of earth in the tap water. Pipes age in silence, and the moment that aging crosses some threshold it bursts into view aboveground as a break or a leak. The trouble is that the threshold differs for every pipe and every segment, varying with the year of burial, the soil, and the water pressure.

The traditional approach was reactive. Fix it when it bursts, patch it when it leaks. But for underground infrastructure the cost of reacting after the fact is unusually high. The excavation itself paralyzes the city's functions, spreads into secondary accidents, and demands an enormous budget to repair. So the question we posed reversed the direction. Could we know before it bursts? From the faint tremors in the data a pipe leaves behind, could we read the timetable of an accident that had not yet come, and read it early?

Pipes age in silence. What we set out to do is to read that silent timetable early, through data.

This question soon took concrete shape as a national R&D project carried out jointly by the City of Daejeon, K-water, and AST Holdings (Jan 2024 – Dec 2026, with a budget of roughly 1.6 billion won). I was responsible for the AI prediction and inference part of the project. The project's very title — "Prediction of Water-Pipe Breaks and Leaks from Irregular Time Series" — was already an honest warning of the difficulties that lay ahead.

Chapter 2. The Challenge of an Irregular Time Series

Clean data existed nowhere

Anyone who has worked with time-series forecasting knows it. The time series in textbooks line up neatly at equal intervals, but real-world time series do not. Water-pipe data was close to the extreme of that "do not." On some days the sensors spat out a value every minute; on others, once a day. Communication failures left whole hours blank, and uncalibrated instruments left behind physically impossible outliers. A large portion of the data we worked with was AIHub-based open assets, but being publicly available and being immediately usable for training are two entirely different stories.

So the first phase of the project was not the model but the data. How should we fill in the missing values — we had to separate the segments where simple linear interpolation was enough from those where filling in that way would instead manufacture a fake trend. How should we filter the outliers — we had to distinguish an abrupt change that was a true precursor to an accident from one that was merely a measurement error. How should we align the non-uniform sampling — we had to minimize the information lost when forcing irregular observations onto an equally spaced grid.

A lesson from the field. We discarded the easy rule "always remove outliers" early on. In waterworks the most valuable signal is often hidden inside the very values that fall outside the normal range. In the end the outlier-handling pipeline was redesigned around "classification and flagging" rather than "deletion" — instead of erasing an outlier, we chose to tell the model that it was one.

Looking back, more than half of the project's total effort went into this front end. Before drawing any dazzling model architecture, the first job was to make the data speak for itself. And the quality of this tedious preprocessing quietly set the ceiling on all the prediction performance that followed.

Chapter 3. Status Prediction — Ensemble Design and a MAPE Comparison

Which model reads a pipe's future best

Once the data was ready, the model experiments began in earnest. From the start we did not bet on a single model. Instead we made candidates of differing character compete under the same conditions. We standardized on MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) as the benchmark metric — because the scale of the values differed greatly from one pipe segment to another, a relative error guaranteed a fairer comparison than an absolute one.

The simplest DNN was fast, precisely because it ignored the temporal order, but shallow. The standard LSTM captured the flow of the sequence; the Stateful-LSTM carried state across batches and so was strong on long-range dependencies; and the LSTM-Attention weighted, on its own, which observation at which point mattered most for the prediction. Below is a summary of the tendencies observed in the experiments — note that the concrete figures are illustrative and vary with the project, the segment, and the evaluation set.

The conclusion of the comparison was not "pick a single winner." Different models exhibited different types of failure. Some were strong on the gentle aging trend; others were sensitive to abruptly changing anomalies. So instead of lining them up in a single rank, we decided to bind them into one team. We combined LSTM, GRU, and Attentive-RNN through ensemble voting, designed so that the weaknesses of the individual models would cancel one another out.

Different models are wrong in different ways. The real value of an ensemble lies not in accuracy but in the diversity of ways it can be wrong.

What we obtained this way was the system's first axis, "break-and-leak status prediction." This layer, which puts out as a probability when and to what degree a given segment will be at risk in the future, was meaningful in its own right. But we did not want to stop here.

Chapter 4. Maintenance Inference — Extending the Answer with a Language Model

The question after "when will it burst"

Suppose the prediction model tells us "the break risk for this segment is high." To the person on the ground this is only half an answer. The next question follows immediately. So what should be done, and how? Replace or repair? Which segment takes priority? Which construction method is appropriate? This realm of prescription was precisely the final bridge that turns the value of a prediction into a field decision.

So we layered a second axis onto the system: a language-generation layer that classifies the damage grade and infers, in sentences, the corresponding maintenance plan. Taking risk level, pipe attributes, and history data as inputs, Bi-LSTM, Transformer, and BERT-family models were made to generate prescriptions in practical, working language — such as "this segment is grade High for deterioration, a priority for repair, and partial rehabilitation should be considered."

This two-stage structure — a design that layers prescription on top of prediction — was the most contested yet most rewarding decision of the project. The reason it was contested is clear: you cannot trust a prescription generated by a language model at face value. So from the very start we nailed down one principle. This system does not replace expert judgment. It assists it. A generated maintenance plan is not the final decision but a grounded draft from which the person in charge begins their review.

An attitude toward safety. In infrastructure where public safety is at stake, AI's role must be clear. Our system's language-model output is always confined to a tone of "recommend and review," and is presented together with the risk-level rationale. Leaving the center of gravity of judgment with the human expert to the very end — this was a design constraint that took priority over convenience.
Chapter 5. Platform Deployment and Implications

Planting the model in the city

There is a wide gap between a model that runs well in the lab and a system that is used in a city. The final phase was to bind these two axes — status prediction and maintenance inference — into one and deploy them as an API on an integrated safety-management (digital-twin) platform. On top of the digital twin, an administrator could now look down on the underground pipe network like a map and check, together, the risk level and recommended maintenance plan for each segment.

There was a reason for the decision to deploy as an API. Rather than embedding the model into one particular screen, opening it up as a service lets it combine flexibly with the platform's other functions and be swapped out behind the scenes as the model improves in the future. Establishing prediction not as a single component but as a continuously updated capability — that was the true intent behind the API form.

The biggest implication this project left me with is that "the completion of a prediction is not the prediction." However accurate a risk number may be, if it is not translated into the field's next action it remains a report in a drawer. Only when all three steps were in place — reading the future with recurrent networks, extending an answer to that future with a language model, and placing it in human hands through the platform — did it finally become a "system."

Closing

A voice for the silent infrastructure

Water pipes will remain hard to see going forward. But that silence does not itself mean safety. This project was an attempt to listen to the underground silence with data as a stethoscope, to read the timetable of breaks and leaks early, and to extend that, in language, to what should be done next. The irregular time series was never fully tamed in the end, and the language model's prescriptions still require human review. Even so, the direction is clear: to have AI watch over the aging of city infrastructure on our behalf, so that people can focus on better judgment. Making the unseen predictable — that, I believe, is the first step toward rewriting this underground timetable.
