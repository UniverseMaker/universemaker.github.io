Drawing People on a Map Without a Camera: mmWave Geo-Referencing

Millimeter-wave radar sees a person not as an image but as a cluster of 'points.' The story of geo-referencing indoors with 3.5 cm average error, then scaling it to a stadium.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST July 2026 · 15 min read
1-min summary For a long time, figuring out where people are indoors was the camera's job. But CCTV-based positioning invades privacy, goes blind in the dark, and—above all—the pixel coordinates on a screen cannot tell you a person's actual position on a map. This piece looks back at a study in geo-referencing: detecting people as a point cloud with a 60 GHz millimeter-wave (mmWave) FMCW radar, clustering those points expressed in spherical coordinates, and then combining them with the sensor's installation data (GIS) to recover real latitude and longitude. In field experiments the average geo-referencing error was about 3.5 cm, a 95.33% improvement in precision over the conventional CCTV approach (APCC 2024, first author). We then scaled a single sensor into a distributed array of many sensors, attached an MQTT pipeline and integrated control, and drew the zone-by-zone occupancy at the Daejeon World Cup Stadium in real time—without a single camera.
Chapter 1. The Problem

The Old Question of “How Many People, and Where”

Anyone who has ever run a large indoor space eventually gets stuck on the same question. Right now, inside this building, in which zone, how many people are there? When a fire breaks out, on which floor are the people who still haven't escaped concentrated? Is a crowd building up dangerously at a particular gate of the stadium? The most familiar way to get answers is the camera. But the camera carries three fundamental limitations.

The first is privacy. Blanketing an entire space with devices that identify people face by face is a bargain—accepting surveillance as the price of safety. The second is physical fragility. When lighting is low, smoke fills the air, or backlight is severe, image recognition collapses rapidly. The very disaster situations in which a person's location matters most are precisely the situations a camera sees worst. The third, and the most often overlooked, is the absence of a coordinate system. Even if a camera tells you “there is a person in the top-left of the frame,” it cannot say where that is on the building's floor plan, or what its latitude and longitude are. A pixel is not a map.

A camera can tell you “where on the screen” a person is, but it can never tell you “where on the map” they are.

The problem I latched onto was exactly this third one. Was there no sensor that could avoid identifying people, stay steady even in the dark, and move its detections directly onto real coordinates on a map? I chose millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar as the candidate, and this piece is a retrospective on how that choice led to an average geo-referencing precision of 3.5 cm.

Chapter 2. How mmWave Works

The Radar Sees a Person as “Points”

Millimeter-wave radar uses radio waves in the 60 GHz band, with wavelengths on the order of a few millimeters. The method we used is FMCW (Frequency-Modulated Continuous Wave): it emits a continuous wave whose frequency is modulated over time like a sawtooth, and reads the frequency difference against the returning signal. From this single measurement, the radar obtains four physical quantities at once—the range to the object (from the frequency difference), the object's velocity (from the Doppler phase shift), and the azimuth and elevation angles (from the phase differences produced by the antenna array).

These four values—range r, azimuth φ, elevation θ, and velocity—are essentially the description of a single point in polar (spherical) coordinates. When a person stands in front of the radar, reflections occur from the many surfaces of the body and dozens of points come back. To the radar, a person is not a face or a silhouette but a cluster of points floating in the air—a point cloud. This very property sidesteps the privacy problem at its root, because what comes back is not “who” but only the geometric information that “something is somewhere, moving at some speed.”

But points scattered across spherical coordinates are awkward to handle as they are. In coordinates where angle and distance are intermingled, even the distance calculation for judging “did these two points come from the same person” is not intuitive. So the first step is a dimensional transformation that projects these points into a Cartesian coordinate system, returning them to the x, y, z of the Euclidean space we know. Only with this conversion from polar to Cartesian coordinates does clustering—“grouping nearby points together”—come about naturally.

Chapter 3. Method

Points into People, People into Coordinates

The core pipeline can be summarized in three steps. Group the point cloud into individual people (clustering), find each person's center, and return that center to real latitude and longitude (geo-referencing). Let's take them one at a time.

1. Point-Cloud Clustering — Separating Multiple People

When there are two people in a space, the point cloud gathers into two groups. Applying density-based clustering to the points converted into Cartesian coordinates, nearby points are bound into a single cluster, and each cluster corresponds to one person. For each cluster, taking the centroid of the points gives that person's representative position, and averaging the Doppler velocities tells you whether that person is moving. The principle stays the same as the number of people grows. When three people are standing, three groups form; each group is wrapped in a bounding box, and the number of points, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and the computed latitude and longitude are recorded together. The more points there are (e.g., 184 vs. 57 vs. 59), the stronger that subject's reflection and the more stable the cluster. This metadata later becomes the basis for judging which cluster to trust.

2. Geo-referencing — Returning Relative Coordinates to Absolute Coordinates

What we have obtained so far is, after all, relative coordinates with the sensor as the origin. A value like “2 m to the right and 3 m forward from the front of the radar” cannot become a point on a map unless we know where and in which direction that radar is installed. So in the final step, the sensor's GIS installation data—the latitude and longitude of the installation point, the installation height, and the direction it faces—is combined with the relative coordinates of the cluster center. Rotating the relative coordinates by the installation bearing and translating them by the installation point recovers each person's position as real latitude, longitude, and altitude. This is geo-referencing. Now each person that emerged from the point cloud becomes a single point on the world map.

Why “recover”? In the forward direction, satellites and maps hand us Earth coordinates. We do the opposite: starting from the relative angles and distances the radar measured from its own viewpoint, we reconstruct Earth coordinates. If the sensor's installation data is inaccurate, this entire reconstruction goes off, so in practice the surveying precision at the time of installation becomes the upper bound on the final precision.
Chapter 4. Validation

The Number 3.5 Centimeters

A method being plausible and a method being actually accurate are entirely different matters. To verify precision, we drew a reference grid on a gymnasium floor and mounted the radar on a tripod. Having a person stand at each known point of the grid, we repeatedly measured the difference between the coordinates the radar computed by geo-referencing and the actual surveyed coordinates. The markings on the floor served as the ground truth.

The result exceeded our expectations. The average geo-referencing error was about 3.5 cm—meaning a person's position can be plotted on a map with an error the size of a single palm. Compared with the CCTV-based approach under the same conditions, precision improved by 95.33%. This work was presented as a first-author paper at APCC 2024 (Bali) and is registered in IEEE Xplore under document number 10767948. Of course, I want to be clear that this number was obtained in a controlled experimental environment. In spaces with severe multipath off the walls, in situations where subjects stand overlapping one another, and at sites where the installation survey is inaccurate, the error can grow larger. Even so, a palm-sized average error was enough to give evidential support to the possibility of registering an indoor space onto a map without using a camera.

If a person can be plotted on a map with an error the size of a single palm, without ever being identified—then safety and privacy are no longer things that must be traded against each other.
Chapter 5. Scaling

From a Single Radar to an Entire Stadium

Once a single sensor gave palm-sized precision, the next question came naturally. What if not one but dozens; not one room but an entire stadium? A single radar's field of view (FOV) is limited to a fan shape. So to cover an entire space, you have to place several units at different points and overlap their fans. Each sensor geo-references the subjects within its own field of view onto latitude and longitude, and the results from the different sensors merge into a single picture on the same absolute coordinate system.

A distributed layout demands a communication architecture. To gather the occupancy data that each of dozens of 60 GHz sensors computes into a center, we needed lightweight, real-time messaging. So we chose a lightweight MQTT-based pipeline. Each sensor (publisher) emits the occupancy information for its zone as a topic, and the integrated Ground Control System subscribes to these and gathers them onto a single screen. We built scaling into the design from the start, so that adding another sensor amounts to nothing more than opening one more topic.

The stage where we actually validated this architecture was the Daejeon World Cup Stadium. We divided the stadium floor plan into zones and overlaid the real-time occupancy of each zone onto a control dashboard. Which zone was crowded and where was emptying out appeared as numbers and colors on the floor plan—without a single camera. It was the moment the original goal—drawing a large-scale indoor distribution of people onto a map in real time while protecting privacy—worked at stadium scale.

Closing

What It Means to Draw People on a Map

What this study left me was not a single number, 3.5 cm. It was a shift in perspective. For a long time we set “seeing people better” as the goal, and so we built sharper cameras. But what safety and operations truly needed was not the face but the location. Once we erased the face and kept only the location, the old dilemma of privacy vanished as a problem in itself. A sensor that sees less gave a more useful answer.

Of course, mmWave is no cure-all. Its precision is bound to the installation survey, and extreme multipath and dense crowds remain difficult homework. This system is a tool to aid human judgment, not a referee that replaces it, and especially when it is used for public safety, that boundary must be kept clear. Even so, I believe the direction is distinct. Drawing exactly where a person is onto a map without identifying them—that one quiet point could become the first coordinate for finding someone in a moment of crisis.
