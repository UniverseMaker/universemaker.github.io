Time Is Performance: Accelerating Simulation with Adaptive Parallelism

City-scale traffic simulation gets slower the more accurate it becomes. A record of APE, in which AI predicts load and distributes computation across 24 cores.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST July 2026 · 12 min read
1-min summary In next-generation AI-fused mobility simulation for metropolitan-scale cities, the biggest bottleneck is not the model's accuracy but its execution time. NextSim, a microscopic traffic simulator, sees its runtime explode as the number of agents and the size of the road network grow, making real-time use impossible on a single thread. We designed a method in which AI predicts the load arising in each simulation cycle and adaptively distributes it across many cores (APE, Adaptive Parallel Execution). On an i9-13900K with 24 cores (32 logical processors), per-cycle times swung wildly from about 4,360 ms to 13,585 ms; the goal is to flatten this load imbalance with adaptive distribution so that core utilization becomes uniform. Against a total run of about 119,131 ms, we aim to cut simulation time by 50%. The point is a single one — in simulation, ‘time’ is performance.
Chapter 1. The Problem

Why Is Simulation Slow?

Traffic simulation means relocating an entire city inside a computer. Hundreds of thousands of vehicles and pedestrians move toward their own destinations, signals change, and at every intersection yielding and congestion are computed moment by moment. Because this kind of microscopic simulation tracks each individual object on the road one by one, it comes closest to reality but carries the highest computational cost. That was precisely the wall we ran into in the next-generation AI-fused mobility simulation project for metropolitan-scale cities (a consortium of the Ministry of Science and ICT and KAIST, 2024.07–2027.12). The computational cost of the NextSim simulator was the bottleneck of the entire workflow.

The root of the slowness lies in a multiplication of scale. As the number of agents (vehicles and pedestrians) grows and the nodes and links of the road network become denser, the interactions to be processed in each simulation cycle grow not linearly but faster than that. At the scale of a large city, imitating one hour of reality can mean waiting hours in front of the machine. The problem is not simply that “it takes a long time.” When a simulation is slower than reality, it is no longer a tool that aids decisions but merely after-the-fact analysis.

When a simulation runs slower than reality, it becomes not a decision-making tool but an after-the-fact report.

At this point a shift in perspective was needed. Until now, the quality of a simulation has largely been discussed in terms of how closely it resembles reality — that is, the model's accuracy. But in settings such as traffic control or policy experiments, where a judgment must be made immediately from the results, how quickly it delivers an answer governs performance just as much as accuracy does. However sophisticated the model, a late answer is worth half as much. Our task was not to make the model more accurate, but to make an already accurate model run on time.

Chapter 2. Anatomy of the Bottleneck

The Real Bottleneck: Load Imbalance

To narrow the symptom of “it's slow” down to a cause, you have to look at the logs. NextSim's console stamps the elapsed time for every cycle. Gathering those numbers, the story emerged. Some cycles finished in about 4,360 ms, while others shot up to 13,585 ms. Within the same simulation, one cycle takes more than three times as long as another. The total run time came to about 119,131 ms.

That variance itself is the true identity of the bottleneck. In microscopic simulation, the amount of computation is not uniform over time. A cycle in which vehicles crowd into a particular area — like the morning rush hour — is heavy, while a quiet cycle is light. Some districts of the road network are dense with intersections and see interactions explode, while others are sparse. In other words, the computational load is uneven along both the time axis and the space axis. Yet if you divide computing resources while ignoring this imbalance, the core handed a light share finishes early and sits idle, while the core handed a heavy share falls behind. The whole cycle always waits for the core that finishes last.

The first law of parallel processing. When a single cycle is split across many cores, that cycle's elapsed time is determined not by the average of the cores but by the slowest core. So “we divided up the work” does not mean “we got faster.” If the shares are uneven, no matter how many cores you add, a single heaviest piece holds the whole thing back. The success or failure of parallelization rests not on the number of cores but on the balance of the load.

In short, the opponent we had to fight was not a “slow simulator” but an “imbalanced load.” Merely moving from a single thread to parallel execution is not enough. As long as the load fluctuates over time and space, unless we read those fluctuations in real time and reallocate resources, most of the gains from parallelization leak away as waiting time.

Chapter 3. The Design

Adaptive Parallel Processing — Predict the Load, Then Divide

The solution is called APE, Adaptive Parallel Execution. The core idea is this: rather than mechanically splitting each cycle into equal parts, predict the computational load of the coming cycle in advance and divide adaptively so that the heavy and light shares are roughly equal across cores. This is where AI comes in. The load does not spike at random. Rules are hidden in observable signals such as the time of day, the elapsed-time pattern of the immediately preceding cycles, and the density of each district. From these signals, the AI estimates “which district will be how heavy in the next cycle” and assigns work to the cores according to that estimate.

Here is where this approach parts ways with static partitioning. Static partitioning cuts the road network once, assigns fixed shares to each core, and is done. When the load shifts over time, that fixed assignment soon becomes an outdated map. Adaptive partitioning, by contrast, asks anew at every cycle, “where is the heavy load right now?” and redraws the shares accordingly. When the load moves from the city center to the outskirts, the resources move with it.

Adding cores is easy. The hard part is making all those added cores finish their work at the same time.

There is one balancing point the design must not miss. The more finely we predict the load and the smaller the pieces we divide it into, the better the balance — but prediction and redistribution themselves consume computation. If the orchestration cost exceeds the time saved by parallelization, the tail wags the dog. So APE aims not for “perfect balance” but for “sufficient balance obtained cheaply.” Keep the prediction light, and redistribute only when the shift in load is significant — this restraint is what decides real-world performance.

Chapter 4. Measurement

On 24 Cores, and a Target of 50%

The experimental setup is an Intel Core i9-13900K with 24 cores (32 logical processors combining performance and efficiency cores). On it, APE's goal is clear. Rather than a picture in which only certain cores glow red while the rest idle in blue, the aim is one in which all cores work evenly and simultaneously. That is exactly the state the parallel-utilization monitor in the cover image is reaching for — thirty-two processor graphs rising and falling together at roughly the same height.

The table below summarizes the essence of the problem in numbers. The bottleneck lies not in the average but in the variance.

ItemObserved valueMeaning
Minimum cycle timeabout 4,360 msA light (quiet) cycle
Maximum cycle timeabout 13,585 msA heavy (congested) cycle

Total run timeabout 119,131 msThe baseline for improvement

The third row of the table is the heart of this study. A variance of more than threefold between cycles also means that the waste recoverable through parallelization is that large. If we can scatter the work of a heavy cycle evenly across many cores and exploit the slack of a light cycle, the sum of the ‘slowest pieces’ that dominated total run time shrinks. The 50% reduction we are aiming for comes from here — not by inventing new computation, but by arranging computation we were already doing without waiting.

Of course, 50% is not an easy number. Prediction error, data synchronization between cores, and the limits of cache and memory bandwidth all eat away at the theoretical ceiling. But the moment we pull the baseline of about 119,131 ms down by half, the simulation becomes a qualitatively different thing. When a scenario that took a whole night finishes in half a day, and half a day shrinks to a few hours, only then does the real-time loop of “run it and judge” become possible.

Chapter 5. Implications

Time Is Performance — Beyond the Model, Toward the System

The message this study delivers goes beyond any individual optimization technique. When we discuss the value of an AI system, we often fix our gaze on the model's accuracy. But in domains like simulation, where the result leads directly to a decision, accuracy alone does not complete performance. However correct the answer, if it arrives late its worth is diminished. Here, time is not a secondary metric but performance itself.

That is why APE is not a pure model problem but a systems-engineering problem. How to predict the load, how to distribute it across cores, and how to hold down the orchestration cost — the balance of these three decides the final speed. Halving execution time while preserving the model's accuracy cannot be solved by one smarter predictor. You have to coordinate the entire system in which prediction, distribution, and synchronization interlock.

This perspective is not confined to traffic. Large-scale physics simulation, agent-based social simulation, real-time digital twins — every system whose computational load fluctuates across time and space shares the same bottleneck. The load is never uniform, and the ability to divide a non-uniform load uniformly is what determines scalability. What APE aims at is not merely speeding up a single simulator, but the broader problem of ‘how to tame a fluctuating load in real time.’

Closing

Designing Away the Waiting

A good simulation begins with resembling reality, but a useful simulation is completed by answering on time. We did not make NextSim smarter. Instead, we rearranged the computation it was already doing so that AI predicts the load and scatters it evenly across 24 cores. The goal is not flashy — eliminate idle cores, reduce the drag of the slowest core, and thereby reclaim half the time. But that half turns the simulation from an after-the-fact report into a real-time decision-making tool. However far models advance, designing ‘when the answer arrives’ remains the engineer's job. Because time is performance.
