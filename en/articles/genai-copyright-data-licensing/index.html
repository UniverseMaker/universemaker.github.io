Mining, Called Learning

Generative AI was built by swallowing the writing, pictures, and code humanity has piled up over centuries. That swallowing stands behind an old shield called fair use. But in 2025, courts and markets began re-measuring the shield. Lawsuits and a $1.5 billion settlement, licensing deals spreading quietly, "content signals" closing the door on the web, and a technology that asks provenance back — this is a calm map of the war over training data.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST August 2026 · 15 min read
1-min summary Large generative models are trained on vast text and images scraped from the web. Whether this mass copying counts as fair use has kept U.S. courts busy for years. In June 2025, one federal court split the question: training a model on lawfully acquired books is "exceedingly transformative" and thus fair use — but downloading and keeping pirated copies is a separate infringement. That case led to a record settlement of about $1.5 billion. The New York Times v. OpenAI suit is still ongoing. As litigation became a risk, the market moved to licensing — though the center of gravity is shifting from "training data" to "live access and attribution." Meanwhile creators are building walls with robots.txt, content signals, and AI-bot blocking, and trying to separate the real from the synthetic with C2PA provenance. Korea has yet to pass either a TDM exemption or a training-data disclosure mandate. This piece maps that terrain — and asks how creators can actually be paid. (Figures for money and deal counts are reported or announced estimates.)
Chapter 1. The Swallowed Library

Fair use, the old shield

When people first met generative AI they were struck by its ability, but the question they soon asked was about its sources. What did this model eat to learn to speak like this? The answer is simple and uncomfortable. Almost everything humanity had put on the web — newspaper articles and novels, blogs and wikis, photographs and illustrations, open-source code and scholarly papers — was scraped at scale and compressed into statistical patterns. "Learning" is a polite word, but to a rights holder it looks a lot like mass copying done without permission.

The shield AI companies raised to justify this copying is fair use under U.S. copyright law. The logic runs like this. A model reads the original not to regurgitate it verbatim, but to learn the statistical structure of language. Because the output is something new and different from the source, the use is transformative, and therefore permitted without permission. The long history of search engines copying web pages in order to index them — recognized as fair use — buttressed the argument.

Behind the polite word "learning" stands the uncomfortable fact of unauthorized mass copying. The question is whether that copying is creation, or extraction.

The other side's logic is no weaker. Creators ask: without my writing, this model could not write like this. Yet that model now competes with me in the very market I depend on. How is that "transformation," and how is it "fair"? What proved especially thorny was where and how the originals used in training were obtained. Properly bought or licensed data is one thing; a whole corpus downloaded from a so-called "shadow library" built to circumvent copyright is another. The very way the source was acquired may already be unlawful. Before you can argue the legitimacy of the learning, the provenance of the raw material is already in question.

Chapter 2. The Scales of the Court

In 2025, the scales began to tip

What turned an abstract debate into a concrete line was the courtroom. On June 23, 2025, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California issued a notable summary-judgment order in Bartz v. Anthropic, a suit brought by authors against Anthropic. The holding splits in two. First, training a large language model on lawfully acquired books is "exceedingly" transformative and qualifies as fair use. Second, however, downloading and retaining pirated copies from shadow libraries is a separate infringement not protected by fair use. Learning is forgiven; stealing the material is not.

This distinction is decisive in practice. What becomes the hinge of liability is not the "act" of training a model, but how the training corpus was obtained. Licensed data, crawled from the open web, or downloaded wholesale as pirated files — legal risk flows very differently along those three paths. Shortly after the ruling, the case led to a settlement of about $1.5 billion (as reported), an unprecedented figure in AI copyright. By reports, Anthropic agreed to pay roughly $3,000 for each of some 480,000 books identified as downloaded from shadow libraries. Even when the learning is deemed fair use, the price of stealing the raw material must be paid separately.

The most symbolic front is still open. In The New York Times (NYT) v. OpenAI and Microsoft, the Times alleges that millions of its articles were copied without permission to train ChatGPT. The defense counters that the use is transformative and protected by fair use. What makes this case important is that what is at stake is not static works like novels or textbooks, but journalism that is refreshed every day. It is the point where a news organization's business model collides head-on with AI's real-time summarizing and search, so the conclusion here spills beyond training into a broader question: how may AI consume the news at all?

It is not a text-only problem. The image front is, if anything, more emotional. The photo archive Getty Images sued the image-generation company Stability AI in the U.K. and the U.S., claiming millions of its images were used in training without permission, and individual illustrators and painters filed class actions arguing that "my style is learned and then used to churn out endless imitations of me." Writing dissolves into the statistics of sentences, but in images a particular artist's style itself is reproduced, so the infringement feels far more direct. The old principle that style is not protected by copyright collides head-on with a creator's intuition that "this is plainly a copy of what is mine." This clash is another unresolved zone that keeps the logic of text precedents from mapping cleanly onto images.

The four counterweights of fair use. When U.S. courts assess fair use, they place four factors on the scale — ① the purpose and character of the use (transformative or commercial), ② the nature of the original work, ③ the amount and substantiality used, and ④ the effect on the market for the original. In the AI-training debate, ① (transformativeness) has largely tilted in AI's favor, but the recent center of gravity is moving toward ④. If a model floods the same market as the original author with substitutes, then however transformative it is, the responsibility for cannibalizing that market remains. The question is shifting from "is it transformative?" to "did it swallow the market?"
Chapter 3. From Lawsuits to Contracts

If fines frighten you, pay the price

Legal uncertainty is a cost. Lose a suit and astronomical damages follow; win, and you still burn years and reputation. A rational company tries to convert that uncertainty into predictable spending. The result is the content-licensing deals that have exploded over the past two years. On the far side of litigation, AI companies and content owners are quietly signing contracts.

Even a glance at reported cases reveals the terrain. OpenAI has been among the most aggressive in striking deals with wire services and news outlets — more than twenty are publicly known. In one case OpenAI reportedly even funded the opening of local newsrooms as part of a multi-year publisher deal. France's Mistral partnered with the AFP news agency to feed its assistant some 2,300 stories a day in six languages. Reddit's deal over access to its data API was reported at around $70 million a year (reported, unconfirmed). What matters more than the precision of the numbers is the direction: data has become an asset with a price.

Data is no longer free raw material lying about. The moment it is priced, the web turns from a library into a marketplace.

The more interesting change is in the character of the contracts. Where early deals were one-off — "hand over the accumulated archive for training" — the recent center of gravity is moving toward live access and attribution and links. Instead, the AI shows sources in its answers and sends traffic back to the original, while receiving a continuous feed of fresh data. It is a way of patching, by contract, the weakness of a static model that freezes once training ends. The table below summarizes this transition.

DimensionGen 1: Training-data dealGen 2: Live-access & attribution deal
What is tradedPast archive (static corpus)Real-time feed + attribution/links
PurposePre-training / fine-tuningGrounding answers / citation
Payment structureMostly lump-sum / flatFlat → usage → dynamic pricing
Content owner's upsideOne-time revenueRecurring revenue + traffic/brand
Trend (as reported)Many early onAttribution/live deals surging (several-fold 2023→2025)

This arrangement is a double-edged sword for content owners. Large publishers and giant platforms have leverage and extract payment. But individual creators with no power to sit at the table — bloggers, illustrators, small presses — are shut out of the same deals. The more the licensing market matures, the more the value of data flows toward those who are already big. So the slogan "make AI pay creators" is right, but unless we also ask to whom that payment returns, it is only half of justice.

Chapter 4. Walls and Fingerprints

The web closes its doors; content engraves a fingerprint

If contracts are an after-the-fact remedy, the creator camp is also building preventive defenses. The first wall begins in a single old file: robots.txt, which records the rules of conduct for web crawlers. Originally a gentlemen's agreement for search engines, it has now become the place to declare a refusal of AI training bots. The number of sites blocking known AI crawlers such as GPTBot and CCBot in this file has surged. I will confess that this journal does the same — even the article you are reading now has, in the site's robots settings, an explicit block on collection by AI training bots. Before my writing becomes some model's raw material, the least I can do is post a sign at the door: "ask permission first."

The trouble is that robots.txt is a request, not a lock. A polite crawler obeys, but nothing technically stops one that ignores it and scrapes anyway. Aiming at this gap, several infrastructure providers offered stronger tools in 2025. Cloudflare, for one, began blocking known AI crawlers by default on new domains that year, and layered a machine-readable policy called "Content Signals" onto robots.txt so that permission could be marked by use case — search indexing, AI input, AI training. It went further with experiments that return "402 Payment Required" to certain AI bots instead of access. The aim is not merely to block unauthorized collection, but to put a price on access.

The idea behind "content signals." Traditional robots.txt could only say "may you scrape or not." Content signals add a layer: "and once scraped, what may it be used for?" You can, for instance, allow search indexing while refusing AI training — attaching different signals to the same page by purpose. But this too is only a declaration, with no force of its own. In the end the wall is complete only when the signal (declaration), the firewall (enforcement), and the license (payment) move together. Norms set the direction; technology enforces it.

The second is not a wall but a fingerprint: provenance technology that engraves into the content itself where it came from and how it was made. At its center is "Content Credentials," created by the C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity). It attaches a cryptographically signed history to an image or video — recording who made it, when, with what tools, whether AI was involved, and every edit since. In 2025 the specification advanced toward becoming an international standard, and some generative models announced they would attach these credentials to their outputs. The goal is to plant, in the content, a label that separates the real from the synthetic, and so to build a basis for trust.

But fingerprints have limits too. Signed metadata can be stripped by a mere screen capture or re-encoding, and watermarks can be erased by editing or conversion, as a run of studies has shown. Provenance can prove the authentic, but it cannot block the forged. In the end it is not an all-purpose lock but a label marking a trustworthy starting point. On top of this comes the variable of synthetic data — training the next model on data a model made, to dodge copyright disputes. It may reduce the provenance problem, but it risks feeding back and amplifying the biases and errors of the original, and the question remains: whose work was the raw material of that synthetic data, after all?

Chapter 5. Korea's Coordinates, and Compensation

Between exemption and disclosure

Move all of this onto Korea's coordinate system and the terrain looks a little different. Where the U.S. handles the problem through fair use — flexible but hard to predict case law — Korea and other civil-law systems specifically enumerate copyright limitations in statute. So the key issue converges on whether to add a text-and-data-mining (TDM) exemption to the Copyright Act. It would permit copying for AI-training purposes under certain conditions; the EU and Japan already have such legislation. In Korea too, amendment bills carrying a TDM exemption have been proposed, but none has yet cleared the National Assembly.

There is a countervailing motion as well. In early 2025 the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism said it would push a copyright-law amendment requiring disclosure of the list of data used in AI training. Two forces interlock: allowing learning (exemption) while demanding transparency (disclosure) in return. But global Big Tech strongly opposes disclosing training data — the moment what was learned is revealed, it becomes both a map for lawsuits and competitive intelligence. Where the balance point between exemption and disclosure is set will decide the fate of a Korean-style regime.

Without exemption the industry withers; without disclosure creation runs dry. The art of regulation lies in where you draw the line between the two.

And finally, the most fundamental question that remains — compensating creators. Licensing is an answer for large content owners, but it never reaches the nameless many. The alternatives under discussion run along a few lines: a collective-management model where, as with music-rights trusts, an organization negotiates and distributes in bulk beyond individual contracts; a fund or levy model that collects a slice of AI-service revenue into a creators' pool; and usage-based compensation that uses provenance technology to trace which data actually contributed and pays accordingly. None is complete, but the shared premise is clear: the fruit of learning must return, in some form, to those who supplied the raw material.

In closing — between mining and farming. "Learning" is both mining and farming. Dig up someone else's land without permission and it is mining; share the harvest with whoever gave the seed and it is farming. Today's copyright-and-licensing war is, in the end, the process of fixing the coordinate between these two. Courts have begun to ask about the provenance of the material, markets have begun to price data, and creators have begun to lock doors and engrave fingerprints. I believe this current is not a shackle slowing AI down but the foundation that makes it sustainable. For if those who supplied the material run dry, the next generation of models will end up feeding on their own shadow and wasting away.

Posting an AI-training-bot block on my own writing is not a struggle to hold data hostage. It is a small proposal of a norm — that we ask "did you seek permission?" before "can we connect?" On the day standards, contracts, and technology answer that question properly, we will at last be able to call the mining farming. Until then, this war is not yet over.
