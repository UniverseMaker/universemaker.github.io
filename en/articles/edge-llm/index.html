Language Models That Infer Without a GPU: Lightweight LLMs and GeoLLM for the Field

On underground-utility sites with no server and no GPU, how can a language model work at all?

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST June 2026 · 14 min read
1-min summary Large language models keep growing, yet the ‘field’ where a model has to do its work keeps shrinking. An underground-utility inspection vehicle, an aging PC in a fire-department situation room, an underground space where the internet cuts out — none of these has a GPU or a stable network. This article begins from a constraint met in the Daejeon Metropolitan City·LX·KAIST consortium’s ‘immersive fire-safety city’ project: an environment where training is done on GPUs but inference has to run on CPU alone. From lightweighting (distillation, quantization, pruning, caching), to the strategy of narrowing the task, to GeoLLM that grounds a model in the data of the land, to variable-layer learning that lifts training efficiency — we look at why a ‘small, sharp model’ is another future for AI.
Chapter 1. Constraints Turn Everything Upside Down

When You Can’t Send a GPU to the Field

Growing a model in the lab is fun. A bigger model, a longer context, more parameters. Every time the benchmark score rises, we believe we have moved closer to a better answer. But if the place that model must leave for is not a data center but ‘the field,’ the story flips entirely.

The Daejeon Metropolitan City·LX·KAIST consortium’s ‘convergence-data-driven immersive fire-safety city’ project that I took part in was one such case. The goal was clear: to predict damage to underground facilities such as water mains, gas pipes, and power conduits, and to have a language generation model propose fitting maintenance measures. The problem lay in where this model had to work. Inspection vehicles, field terminals, aging situation-room PCs — once training was finished, inference had to run on CPU alone, without a GPU.

This single line of constraint changes the entire design. In a cloud environment, “let’s make the model bigger” is a reasonable opening move. But in an underground environment with no GPU, tight power, and a dropping network, that option is closed off from the start. Here, “smaller and faster” is not an optimization that concedes performance but a requirement — without it, nothing runs at all.

In the field, “smaller and faster” is not an optimization that shaves performance but a requirement — without it, nothing runs at all.

The moment ‘a bigger model’ becomes the wrong answer arrives more often than you would think. Cost (running an inference server), power (a field terminal’s battery), connectivity (communication blackouts underground or in disasters), and response latency (a situation room cannot afford to wait several seconds) all collide head-on with model size. A good engineer looks not for “the smartest model” but for “the smallest model that is smart enough within these constraints.”

Chapter 2. The Techniques That Make CPU Inference Possible

Making It Small Is a Matter of Taking Away

Running a large model at practical speed on a CPU is not magic. It is engineering that combines a handful of well-known techniques to suit the nature of the task. The key is to shrink while measuring ‘what can be removed without the answer wavering.’

1. A Smaller Model, and Knowledge Distillation

The most direct approach is to use a small model from the start. But a merely small model often falls short. So we use knowledge distillation. When you train a small ‘student model’ by treating the outputs of a large, smart ‘teacher model’ as ground truth, the small model absorbs much of the large model’s judgment tendencies. In a narrow output space like our project’s ‘underground-facility damage type → maintenance phrasing,’ a distilled compact model comes surprisingly close to the original’s quality.

2. Quantization: Lowering Precision to Cut Weight

Representing a model’s weights as 8-bit — sometimes 4-bit — integers instead of 32-bit floating point sharply reduces memory and computation. Because CPUs are strong at integer arithmetic, quantization pays off especially in GPU-less environments. The price is a slight loss of accuracy, and in narrow tasks that loss often stays within practical limits.

3. Pruning, Caching, and Narrowing the Task

Pruning cuts low-contribution connections to make the model sparse. Caching reuses the intermediate computations of recurring input patterns to shorten response time. But the most underrated lever lies elsewhere: narrowing the task. Rather than trying to build an all-purpose assistant, if you focus on the single job of “writing an underground-facility damage report in a standard format,” the model capacity you need shrinks in itself.

Practical tip. Don’t lightweight all at once; apply one technique at a time and, each time, measure accuracy and latency together on the same evaluation set. For instance, if accuracy falls below the threshold after 4-bit quantization, ‘mixed precision’ — rolling back only the sensitive layers to 8 bits — is often the answer. If you don’t know what you lose when you take something away, lightweighting becomes a gamble.

Chapter 3. GeoLLM — A Language Model Grounded in the Land

Making It Reason About Places, Not Just Text

If lightweighting is a matter of ‘small,’ GeoLLM is a matter of ‘sharp.’ A general language model learns the world’s text. So when asked “is the risk for this area’s underground facilities high?” it runs the danger of producing a plausible but ungrounded sentence — because what the model has seen is ‘words,’ not ‘the land.’

GeoLLM is research that grounds a language model in spatial and land data. It connects structured, spatial data — parcel numbers, zoning, the burial depth and deterioration of underground facilities, relationships with adjacent facilities, ground characteristics — directly to the model’s reasoning. The model then answers on the basis of the actual data of that place rather than the statistical average of text. Reasoning whose grounds can be traced becomes possible, of the form: “this segment is a cast-iron pipe buried more than 30 years ago and has a history of adjacent excavation, so it is a priority for inspection.”

Here an important insight emerges. In narrow tasks, domain grounding yields a bigger gain than model size. In a limited domain like underground-facility maintenance, connecting a model accurately to high-quality spatial data raises the accuracy rate more than multiplying the parameters tenfold. And a model grounded in its domain is allowed to be small. Grounding becomes the very justification for lightweighting. ‘Small’ and ‘sharp’ do not conflict; they help each other.

Chapter 4. Variable-Layer Learning

Not Every Input Needs the Same Depth of Thought

If lightweighting is about shrinking a finished model, variable-layer learning is an attempt to raise efficiency from the training stage itself. The core intuition is simple: does a neural network really need to use every layer, for every input, the same way every time?

Easy inputs can be judged well enough with only the shallow layers, and only hard inputs need the deep layers brought in. Variable-layer learning flexibly adjusts this ‘depth used’ according to the input and the training situation. As a result it cuts unnecessary computation so training runs faster, and the role of each layer grows clearer, tending even to improve generalization performance.

Why this idea fits field models well is clear. Better training efficiency yields a better-trained model from the same resources, and a structure that at inference time handles ‘easy inputs shallowly’ leads directly to a shorter average response time in CPU environments. The techniques for making it small and the techniques for training it smartly point to the same place.

Closing

The Vast Cloud and the Small, Sharp Edge

When we picture AI’s future, we often look in only one direction: ‘a bigger cloud model.’ But the place a model actually has to work is not only the data center. A GPU-less inspection vehicle, an underground where communication drops, a situation room that cannot wait even a few seconds — at these edges, the answer is not a giant model but a small, sharp model.

Predicting underground-facility damage and drafting maintenance phrasing does not require a model that knows all the world’s text. It only needs to be grounded in the data of that place, run fast on a CPU, and be accurate enough. The vast cloud and the small edge do not compete. They are two different paths by which AI reaches the world, and a good engineer first reads which path the task demands. The future belongs not only to bigger models but also to small models that have read their constraints precisely.
