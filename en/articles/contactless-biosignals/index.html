Reading Biosignals Without Contact: Smart Mirrors and Immersive Content

PPG that measures your pulse with a camera alone, and the story of digital signage that recognizes people.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST June 2026 · 12 min read
1-min summary Every time the heart beats, blood surges beneath the skin, and with it the skin's color ripples so faintly that the human eye cannot see it. Photoplethysmography (PPG) is the principle of reading exactly this tremor of color to estimate the pulse. By mounting a camera on a Raspberry Pi-based smart mirror, we built a system that estimates the pulse contactlessly from the face of a person standing in front of the mirror. The same terminal also carried an interactive digital signage that recognizes the context of the person in front and changes its content accordingly, machine-learning-based content management, and a dual-screen signage built from heterogeneous devices (a registered patent). This piece gathers what we learned wrestling with light and noise as that "immersive content terminal" made its way from a lab demo to a mirror in the hallway.
Chapter 1. An Almost Magical Idea

The pulse surfaces on the skin as color

Blood absorbs light. Hemoglobin, in particular, drinks in green wavelengths readily. When the heart beats once, blood surges into the capillaries, and in that instant the skin absorbs a little more light. As the blood recedes, the absorption drops again. So the brightness of the skin rises and falls faintly in step with the rhythm of the heart. It is a change on the order of a few dozenths, something the human eye can never notice. Yet a camera's sensor can catch that tremor as numbers.

Photoplethysmography, or PPG, is a principle familiar from the pulse oximeters that clip onto a fingertip. There, an LED and a photosensor measure how much light passes through and reflects off the skin between them. What we did was lift the "contact" out of that measurement. Instead of a dedicated sensor, we used an ordinary camera; instead of touching the skin, we tracked how the pixel values of the facial region change over time. If you pick the areas where skin is well exposed on the face (the forehead, the cheeks) and record the average color frame by frame, the period of the heartbeat is hidden inside that signal. Pull that period out of the noise with signal processing, and the pulse rate surfaces without strapping anything to the wrist.

Every time the heart beats, the skin's color ripples faintly. People cannot see it, but the camera catches that tremor as numbers.

Why insist on being contactless? The first reason is hygiene. Passing the same sensor around in a public space where many people come and go always carries a sense of aversion. If nothing touches, that burden disappears. The second is everydayness. The moment you have to "wear" something to take a measurement, it becomes a medical act; but if your pulse is quietly read during the thirty seconds you brush your teeth in front of the mirror, it becomes part of everyday life. The third is accessibility. Someone clumsy with devices, someone whose hands are impaired, need only stand in front of it. Lowering the threshold of measurement was, in effect, returning the act of watching over one's health to more people.

Chapter 2. The Reality of Engineering

Why the demo is smart but the hallway mirror is dumb

The more elegant the idea, the harsher the reality. In a controlled lab, under good lighting, measuring the pulse of someone sitting still and gazing into the camera is not hard. The trouble comes next. The mirror hangs in a hallway. People do not hold still. The lighting flickers under fluorescent tubes, or shifts moment by moment with the sunlight coming through a window. The signal that was crisp in the demo sinks into noise in the field.

The biggest enemies were three: motion, lighting, and noise. When a person turns their head or changes expression, the brightness of the facial region swings wildly, quite apart from the heartbeat. This change has a far larger amplitude than the heartbeat signal, and left alone it completely buries the real signal. Lighting is the same. The 50/60 Hz flicker of fluorescent lamps, the shadow of a passer-by, changes in light level over the course of the day all manufacture false periodicities.

Engineering note. Robustness came not from a single clever trick but from several layers of defense. (1) Face detection re-grabbed the region of interest every frame so it would follow the small movements of the head; (2) a band-pass filter that passes only the frequency band where a heartbeat can exist (roughly 0.7–4 Hz, i.e. 42–240 beats per minute) shaved off the lighting noise; (3) combining multiple color channels to separate the motion component from the pulse component raised the signal-to-noise ratio. (4) Above all, in stretches where confidence was low, the system honestly displayed Measuring… and refused to output a value. Silence is better than a wrong number.

The most important design decision was that last item. Contactless measurement is, by its very nature, not a technology that can always take a reading. While a person moves, while the light wavers, it cannot be trusted. So we steered away from "a system that always shows a number" toward "a system that quietly reports only when it can measure." Once we had it evaluate the stability of the signal itself and display a result only when a certain threshold was crossed, the trust users felt finally rose. Accuracy was not only the error in the number, but also the honesty of knowing to say "I'm wrong" when it is wrong.

What became clear as I laid out this table is that contactless measurement is not a technology that "replaces" the contact kind. It occupies a different seat. Clinical settings that demand precision are still the domain of contact measurement. The real value of contactless measurement lay in slipping gently into a moment no one had ever measured before — the ordinary thirty seconds of a morning spent in front of the mirror — and watching over the texture of one's health "on an always-on basis."

Chapter 3. Signage That Recognizes the Person in Front

A screen that never demands attention, but notices first

The same terminal had another axis: interactive digital signage. Ordinary signage plays fixed content one-directionally. What we set out to build was a screen that recognizes the context of the person in front and changes its content on its own. Who is standing there, in what situation, and how close — depending on that context, what it shows changes. To someone far away it offers a large-type, attention-catching screen; to someone who has come close it extends a detailed interaction.

Here machine learning worked in two places. One was context-aware recommendation — learning from accumulated data which content responds well to which context, so that when a similar context comes next it picks something more fitting. The other was smart content management — having the model organize when and where a great many pieces of content had worked well, so that it could propose a suitable arrangement without the operator scheduling each one by hand. This was the point where signage changed from a mere "player" into a "screen that judges."

A good screen never demands attention. When a person approaches, it notices first; when they leave, it quietly withdraws.

The most technically demanding part was the dual-screen signage built from heterogeneous devices. It is the task of binding two devices with different performance and characteristics — say, a lightweight embedded device and a high-performance display — into a single content experience. One screen senses the context while the other displays rich content, yet the two had to keep time together without a hitch. This structure, which temporally synchronizes devices with different operating systems and processing speeds and divides their roles, was consolidated into a registered patent. What mattered more than the patent itself was the design thinking of making cheap and expensive devices look like one even while mixing them — that is, of bridging real-world budgets and ideal experiences.

Chapter 4. The Thread That Runs Through It All

Making computing quiet and humane

A mirror that reads the pulse, and signage that recognizes people. On the surface they look like different projects, but the thread running through both was one: computing that does not demand attention. Good technology does not shout at the user, "Look at me, operate me." Stand in front of the mirror and your pulse is quietly read; step up to the screen and the fitting content surfaces first. People live as they always do, and the technology senses and responds from the background.

This is commonly called "ambient computing." The idea is that instead of computation being confined inside a particular device, waiting for the user's command, it seeps into the environment, reads context, and quietly lends a hand. Contactless measurement and context-aware signage were two faces of the same philosophy. The former "senses without touching," the latter "responds without demanding." Both were attempts to lift a burden off of people.

But this direction carries an ethical responsibility. A technology that senses is also a technology that watches. So we set a principle from the start. The video itself is not stored; only the signal needed for measurement is extracted at once and then discarded. Recommendation stops at reading "context" rather than "identifying" any individual. For quiet computing not to become surveillance, we had to design first what it would not sense.

Closing

On building for the field, not for the demo

The biggest lesson this project left me was less technical than attitudinal. The lab demo and the hallway mirror are utterly different beings, even while sharing the same code. A demo needs only to show that it "works" under the best conditions, but the field must prove that it "does not collapse" under the worst. The distance between them is filled not by the sophistication of the algorithm but by how you handle failure.

What we were ultimately proud of in the contactless measurement was not the accuracy number, but the honesty of quietly holding its tongue when it could not be trusted. What mattered in the signage was not flashy interaction, but the design that mixed cheap and expensive devices to create a single experience within real constraints. Building for the field was not about lowering one's ideals, but about honing those ideals to endure even amid the noise of reality. If someone who paused for a moment in front of the screen was quietly helped without even being aware of what they were facing — with that, I believe this work has done its part.
