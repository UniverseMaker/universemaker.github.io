Where, and What Happened

The language of a disaster scene arrives as an address — "somewhere in that district, the basement of a shopping arcade." But the judgement is made from video and sensors: the thickness of the smoke, the slope of the water level, the period of the shaking. Between those two languages sits a gap nobody fills for you. This year I built two pieces of software, one on each side of it — a knowledge graph that resolves addresses into coordinates and relations, and a perception API that reads four modalities on a single backbone. This is a record of trying to make them one stack, and of what is still undone.

DP
Daeseung Park AI & Software Systems Engineer · KAIST August 2026 · 17 min read
1-min summary Input to a disaster-response system almost always arrives as an address string, yet the actual judgement is made from video and sensor signals. Bridging those two is the subject of this piece. The lower layer is AddressKG — a knowledge graph built on Neo4j by decomposing Korean road-name addresses into nodes and relations according to the national standard ontology. The operational graph, loaded with the full address data of two metropolitan regions, holds 1,682,015 nodes and 5,527,976 relations, and every node and edge carries a deterministic identifier, GSID, so that the same sources always reproduce the same graph. The upper layer is the Urban Safety AI API — serving software that reads video, sensor time series, geospatial data, and text on one LLM backbone to recognise fire, flood, and collapse, and that validates every inter-organisation boundary against JSON Schema. The moment I tried to join the two, the real problem turned out not to be the model but the join key. How do you declare that the same building is the same building, when one layer is dense with 54 standard classes and 73 relations and the other is deliberately coarse at 12 node types and 8 relations? This piece is about that seam, about a design that will not produce a single sentence without evidence, and about the homework that remains. (Figures in the body are measured values from a July 2026 rebuild rehearsal; unverified performance numbers are deliberately absent.)
Chapter 1. Two Languages

Reports come as addresses; judgement comes as scenes

Look at disaster-response software long enough and an odd asymmetry stands out. The door through which an incident enters the system is almost always an address. Intake, dispatch, evacuation notices, after-action reports — the way humans convey a location to other humans is a string of road name and building number. Yet the material the system actually uses to decide is of an entirely different kind: frames from a camera, a time series from a water-level gauge, a spectrum from a vibration sensor. The first is the language of meaning; the second is the language of signal.

The two do not translate into each other automatically. A camera does not know which building and which floor it is looking at, and an address database does not know that smoke is rising from that building right now. So in the field a person performs the translation. An operator watches the screen, calls up a map in their head, and concludes: "that is probably near exit three of the underground arcade." This translation is remarkably accurate and remarkably unscalable. It works with ten screens. It does not work with two hundred.

Incidents arrive as addresses and judgements are made from signals. As long as the translation between them lives inside someone's head, the system scales only as far as the number of screens.

This year I owned one piece of software on each side of that asymmetry. One is AddressKG: decomposing road-name address data into nodes and links and standing it up as a knowledge graph. The other is the Urban Safety AI API: serving software that reads video, sensors, geospatial data, and text together on one large-language-model backbone to recognise a disaster situation, and hands the result out in a form several organisations can exchange. At first the two looked like separate jobs in separate drawers. But once each was partly built, looking back, they were the two clauses of a single sentence — the layer that answers where, and the layer that answers what happened.

This piece is a record of trying to stand those two layers up as one pipeline. It is less a success story than a record of the seam, because nearly every interesting problem turned out to live not inside either layer but between them.

Chapter 2. The Lower Layer

Resolving an address into relations

An address looks like a string but is really a tree. Province, then district, then road name, then building number. On top of that, legal and administrative subdivisions overlay each other along different axes, and national base zones cover it along yet another. Push that structure into relational tables and lookups work, but questions like "which legal subdivisions does this road pass through?" or "what is next to this building?" require a new join every time. So I went with a graph.

The single source of truth for the design is not the code but a standard document. The ontology of the national "Address Information Knowledge Graph Model" standard (Parts 1–3) sits above everything, and the web edition of the specification, the RDF/OWL, the JSON-LD context, the SHACL shapes, and the data dictionary are all generated from that one source. The path by which documentation and implementation could diverge was removed outright. The graph splits along two axes: a Core Network (HSM) holding address elements normalised into standard classes together with hierarchical, spatial, and semantic relations, and an External Network (ESC) that attaches public-dataset records to those address elements. Along the way, revising the treatment of "object addresses" from thirteen mapped types to twenty-five individual classes grew the class count from 42 to 54 and the relation count from 61 to 73.

Working with a standard taught me one rule of discipline: when you need a concept the standard lacks, do not quietly enlarge the standard. Entrances, the relation by which an administrative subdivision governs a legal one, the relation by which a road passes through a subdivision, the entity-resolution relation asserting that two records refer to the same thing — all of these went into a separate extension registry. And from that registry, a standard-revision proposal is generated automatically and flows back toward the standard. Acknowledge the need, prevent the contamination, return it as a proposal. If those three beats are not enforced by tooling, then one night someone will slip in an extra class.

The same sources must yield the same graph. This graph's identifier, GSID, is issued not by hashing but by a deterministic rule — a domain code, an S2 Level-16 spatial cell, an above/below-ground tier, a generation, a deterministic sequence number, and a Luhn mod-36 check character. A single building number becomes, for example, BD-357CA3FE-G0-00-913547-I. It is assigned not only to nodes but to edges as well. Because every load step is idempotent, re-running the pipeline — or rebuilding from scratch on a different machine — yields the same identifiers. When regions are added sequentially, previously issued GSIDs are adopted as-is and only collisions are re-issued, after which edge endpoints are remapped so that identifiers stay immutable. This tedious-sounding rule turns out to be the only foundation stone under the "join key" problem below.

Coordinates come not from the text database but from address geometry files. The source coordinate reference system (EPSG:5179) is converted during ingestion into WGS84 and EPSG:5186 and injected into the nodes, and the S2 cell is then recomputed from the real coordinates so the GSID is re-issued. Once coordinates are present, spatial relations can be computed: adjacency, which road a building fronts, what lies within a radius, which zone contains which building. These relations are produced not by hard-coded queries but by a declarative rule engine. The judgement that a building fronts a road is grounded in the actual contact point between an entrance and a road, falling back to the road that assigned the address only when no contact data exists — and lowering the confidence value when it does. Containment cross-validates spatial inclusion against subdivision codes. Rules can be registered, edited, disabled, recomputed, and deleted from the web console, and every operation is written to an audit log.

Public-data linkage is a pull model. Declare a dataset in the catalogue and the system attempts links in order — address code join (confidence 1.0), then coordinates (0.9), then area (0.8) — and if none of them attach, it creates no link and preserves only the record. That is the design of someone who knows a forced link will later eat away at trust in the entire graph. The sources actually linked are 94,030 commercial-district records (78,607 and 15,423 across the two regions, 99.6% linked by coordinates), 1,175 Wikidata POIs, and from national standard datasets 18,530 car parks plus 11,605 libraries, museums and galleries, and publicly opened facilities, along with 857 local-government invested and funded institutions. The last of those had no coordinates. Rather than bolting on an external geocoding service, they were geocoded by the graph's own building-number code join. Once a graph is dense enough, the graph becomes the geocoder.

A knowledge layer sits on top of that. An address glossary is parsed into term nodes with semantic-matching edges; a 560-page operational handbook is mined for terms, question-and-answer rulings, operating criteria, a chapter-and-section tree, and body chunks — extracted verbatim, with no summarising or rewriting — and materialised as nodes; legal citations become their own nodes with citation relations. Search is a fallback chain of exact match, partial match, token AND, alias, and finally vector KNN, where the vectors come from a locally hosted embedding model (768 dimensions). Running with no paid API, offline, at zero cost was a requirement from the outset.

Chapter 3. The Upper Layer

Reading the scene

The problem statement in the upper layer is the mirror image of the lower one. Here the data is abundant and the formats disagree. Video is a lattice of frames, sensors an axis of time, geospatial data coordinates and topology, text a stream of tokens. For one model to read all four, they must first be made the same shape. So the first decision was not an architecture but a contract: all four encoders implement the single abstract interface encode(inputs) → Tensor[B, T, D]. Honour the contract and you can add a new modality without touching the projection or the training loop.

The projection that aligns encoder output with the LLM's embedding space can be swapped through a factory among linear, MLP, and cross-attention variants. Training is split into four stages — an encoder-only baseline, projection alignment, joint fine-tuning with LoRA, and supervised fine-tuning for disaster recognition. Each stage declares its entry, abort, and rollback criteria, and a shared callback that periodically measures general-capability retention is a mandatory component.

Why the backbone is barely touched. Fully fine-tuning a large language model on domain data yields something that reads disaster documents well while its general Korean ability collapses — catastrophic forgetting. So the roles were divided. Encoders are frozen by default; modality alignment belongs to the projection layer; domain adaptation belongs to LoRA adapters; knowledge enrichment comes from retrieving a spatial knowledge graph and injecting it as context. If retention drops below a configured threshold, the training script lowers the learning rate or halts and rolls back to the previous checkpoint. Adding capability and not losing capability are two separate processes, and the second one is always forgotten unless it is automated.

On the serving side, the most practical decision was making the inference backend swappable by a single string. A mock, a rule-based local engine, direct transformer loading, a commercial API, and a high-throughput inference engine — five kinds behind the same interface. As a result the API paths, latency measurements, and reports can be exercised for real with no GPU and no actual model weights; for deployment you only change the backend. In research software, the moment someone can say "we can't proceed because the model isn't ready," the schedule is gone. Making that sentence impossible was the serving layer's first job.

The same instinct was applied to boundaries between organisations. In a programme where several organisations build their own parts in parallel, work stalls easily on the excuse that a counterpart module does not exist yet. So the message format of every boundary was nailed down in JSON Schema first — edge device to model, retrieval layer to model, model to serving and validation platforms — and external integrations were isolated behind adapters so the whole path could be traversed with mocks alone. The gateway validates input and output against those schemas at each boundary and rejects violations with a 422. Specification breaches surface at development time rather than at integration time.

The end-to-end endpoint traverses eight steps in a single call — sensor input, retrieval of grounding evidence, mapping between the spatial knowledge graph and 3D objects, assembling the inference request, the model response, endpoint registration, validation-evaluation binding, and the integrated situation board — checking on the spot that each step's output satisfies the relevant boundary schema. Streaming the same pipeline over server-sent events makes per-step progress and latency observable live in a browser. Latency, too, is not something measured afterwards: it is wired from the start into a measurement endpoint on the gateway, so any backend is measured the same way.

And the data layer carries exactly one strong rule: data without provenance, licence, and de-identification metadata cannot enter training. Schema validation filters it out. Disaster data, by its nature, captures people and captures addresses. Making that rule a gate in code rather than a recommendation in a document is a concession to the knowledge that recommendations are always yielded once a deadline approaches.

Chapter 4. The Seam

Harder than the model: the join key

When I actually tried to join the two layers, the wall appeared somewhere I had not expected. I had assumed the problem would be on the model side — how to embed spatial information, what dimension to align graph embeddings to. But the blockage came earlier. The two layers cannot call the same building the same building.

The reason is simple and structural. The lower layer takes a national standard as its source of truth, so its vocabulary is dense: 54 classes, 73 relations, with building numbers, entrances, base segments, and national base zones each a distinct class. The upper layer has a different purpose. It keeps only what safety reasoning needs, coarsened to 12 node types (building, parcel, road, address, shelter, transit stop, park, CCTV, sensor, river, zone, exit) and 8 relations (near, adjacent to, connected to, located in, monitors, evacuates to, has exit, serves). The coarse side is not lazy. To compute a safety score you must have shelter, transit, park, and monitoring-device nodes — and the standard address vocabulary has no such concepts to begin with.

That the two graphs differ in vocabulary granularity is not the defect. The defect is that there is no crosswalk between them.

On top of that sits the identifier problem. Node identifiers in the upper layer are being discussed as a prefix convention (distinguishing buildings, zones, shelters, stops, monitoring devices, and so on by prefix), and that convention is not yet settled across the organisations involved. You cannot write a join against an unsettled key. The lower layer, by contrast, already has a deterministic, immutable GSID; that GSID carries a spatial cell inside it; and it is attached to edges as well as nodes. Three reasons make it a strong candidate for the seam. First, the same sources yield the same value, so the two layers agree even when each computes independently. Second, because it embeds coordinates, spatial-proximity queries can first narrow down approximately correct candidates. Third, the check character catches a key mangled in transit immediately.

In fact the two layers already share several things. The coordinate reference system is the same — the source is EPSG:5179, and the 3D mapping message carries coordinates and CRS along with the payload. The geographic area they cover is the same. And the upper layer's 3D/BIM mapping schema already bundles, for one safety-graph node, a 3D object identifier, a BIM GUID, coordinates, and an indoor level of detail — that is, the slot for carrying a key already exists in the specification. What is missing is a source for the value that goes in it. That the lower layer geocoded 857 institutions with no coordinates using nothing but its own graph is, in effect, its own audition for that role.

Chapter 5. Saying "I Don't Know"

Not one sentence without evidence

Mention using a language model in the disaster domain and the first reaction is always "what if it makes things up?" That is a fair worry. When a peacetime chatbot is wrong, the user laughs it off; when a control-room system confidently states the wrong address, a fire engine turns down the wrong alley. Building the address agent in the lower layer, I reframed the problem from "reduce hallucination" to "make it impossible to produce a sentence without evidence."

The pipeline is Graph-First / LLM-Last. A deterministic natural-language-understanding stage resolves intent and entities, and read-only tools execute only pre-defined parameterised queries to gather facts. Then, when a sentence is generated, the absence of an identifier for the node that grounds it raises a citation error. The generated sentence is verified by comparing its numbers and names against the collected facts down to digit boundaries; on failure the sentence is discarded and the system returns to a deterministic fallback. One more layer sits on top: the agent object refuses a writable backend with a type error in its constructor. The path by which a model-generated sentence could enter the graph is blocked at the level of types.

Holding to that principle required measurement, not a feeling that things "seem to work." So six layers of quality gate were built: unit and regression tests, a golden set of measured scenarios, a metamorphic runner that phrases one query five ways and checks that all of them converge on the same entity, a self-query sweep that takes a stratified sample per label and asks the graph about each item by its own name to see whether it finds itself, multi-turn conversation scenarios, and constraint validation. In July 2026 I wiped the database entirely and ran a rehearsal reproducing every step of the runbook. The whole process reproduced, core ingestion included at 596 seconds, and the gates came out perfect — 418 tests, golden 50/50, metamorphic 126/126, self-query 800/800, scenarios 16/16, and zero violations across a 1,474-item constraint-validation sample. The operational graph stood at 1,682,015 nodes and 5,527,976 relations.

More important than the perfect score is how it was reached. The gates were not decoration; they actually caught defects. The metamorphic runner started its first run at 75% and reached 100% only after surfacing five grade-A defects. The self-query sweep started at 69.3% and hit 100% once a full-text exact-match probe and three text indexes were introduced — with the side benefit that the golden set's median response time fell from 590 ms to 165 ms, a 3.6× improvement. The golden set even corrected itself: items that had been passing by coincidence after being hijacked by a different place name had their expectations rewritten to an honest non-resolution. The rebuild rehearsal itself caught four real defects that would never have surfaced without running it.

The gates are not valuable because they scored perfectly. They are valuable because roughly ten defects were caught on the way to that score.

The same yardstick was applied to the language model itself. A small local model was attached as a fallback for natural-language understanding and measured A/B. The deterministic stack already resolved 17 of 20 hard queries; the language model produced zero rescues, zero regressions, and zero effective interventions, while median latency degraded to 31 seconds. So the default was kept at "no language model," and the conditions for re-evaluation were written down: a dedicated accelerator separate from embedding, a measured rise in the non-resolution rate, and a faster small Korean model. Deciding not to use a language model should also be a decision made from data.

This layer does not replace judgement. What the upper layer produces is a situation type, a severity, and the grounds for that assessment. Outcomes that bear directly on human safety — identifying evacuation blind spots, for instance — were specified from the design stage as subject to review by field experts, and the weights are injected from configuration rather than baked into code so that they remain adjustable. The lower layer is the same: it answers with evidence attached, or it answers that it does not know. In disaster response the role of software is not to make the decision but to put better material into the hands of the person deciding, faster. Not crossing that line is a shared design constraint of both layers.
Chapter 6. What Remains

Writing down what is not done

I have used the word "stack," but the two layers are not fully joined into one pipeline today. Let me set down honestly what remains. First, the vocabulary crosswalk exists only as a document. The node and relation mapping between the layers is a draft, and until the upper layer's identifier convention is settled no automated join can be written. The best available move right now is to leave the slot for the key open in the specification and finish getting the lower layer ready to produce the value.

Second, there are no performance figures yet for the upper layer. The latency-measurement path and the report format exist, but the stage of measuring disaster-recognition accuracy with real model weights has not begun. That is why this piece contains not one number about recognition performance. Dressing a missing number up as "approximately" or "roughly" is not measurement; it is fiction.

Third, geographic scope and update cadence remain open. The lower layer has loaded the full data of only two metropolitan regions, and incremental updates are handled with manifests, watermarks, and tombstones, with recomputation deliberately restricted to the changed set and its spatial neighbours — a global recomputation is treated as a defect and caught. Holding data that moves quarterly and sensors that move by the second in the same graph would take one more design decision about where the time axis belongs. That one has no answer yet.

Even so, working through both efforts side by side left me with one conviction. In disaster response the bottleneck for artificial intelligence is usually not the model but the seam. As long as two systems covering the same city cannot call the same building by the same name, even an excellent perception model can only say "there is a fire somewhere." Conversely, once the seam is settled, an ordinary model can say "near this exit of this building, a situation of this kind, at roughly this severity." That second sentence is the one that gets used in the field.

Closing — the moment a graph becomes a geocoder. When 857 institutions with no coordinates could be geocoded by the graph's own code join, with no external service, I came to understand the nature of this work a little differently. An address knowledge graph is not a database for lookups; it can be a reference frame that fixes the position of other systems. That is exactly what the perception layer above needs. The model brings the ability to read a scene, but somebody has to establish where in the world that scene is. This is why the two layers must meet as one stack. Perception answers "what happened," and the address graph answers "where." And the sentence a disaster scene needs is always the one that holds both in a single breath.
